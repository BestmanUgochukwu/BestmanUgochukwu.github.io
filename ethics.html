Ethical Dilemma Analysis

Examining professional ethics through the lens of faith and IT responsibility

Case Study

Misuse of Admin Privileges

The Violation
Unauthorized access to confidential student records by a system administrator
Those Affected
The sysadmin, affected students, and the institution's IT department
Initial Response
Access revoked, investigation launched, stricter IAM policies implemented

📋 Situation Analysis

A system administrator at an educational institution exploited their elevated access privileges to view confidential student records without authorization or legitimate business need. This type of violation — often called "privilege abuse" or "insider threat" — is one of the most serious ethical breaches in IT because it weaponizes trust. The sysadmin was granted administrative access to perform legitimate maintenance duties, not to surveil student data. The misuse was a deliberate choice to exploit technical capability beyond its authorized scope.

This case involves multiple simultaneous failures: a breach of confidentiality, a violation of acceptable use policy, a breach of professional trust, and potential violation of FERPA. The sysadmin's actions were not a technical error — they were a moral choice that revealed a fundamental misalignment between technical power and professional integrity.

⚖️ Ethical & Professional Standards Examined

  • Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP): The administrator had access far beyond what their role required. Proper IAM policy would have scoped their access to only the systems they needed — not student record databases.
  • CompTIA Code of Ethics: CompTIA's ethical standards require IT professionals to protect the privacy and integrity of data they are entrusted with. Unauthorized access to personal data is an explicit violation.
  • FERPA Compliance: Accessing student records without educational justification violates federal law in the U.S. The institution bore legal risk the moment this access occurred.
  • Duty of Care: IT professionals in institutional roles hold a duty of care to the people whose data they manage. Students trusted the institution — the sysadmin breached that chain of trust.

✝️ Spiritual & Personal Reflection

This case carries deep meaning for me personally as Technology Coordinator for The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, managing 50+ devices across 10 units. The sysadmin had the technical ability to access those records — ability is not authority. The gap between what you can do and what you should do is exactly where integrity lives. Matthew 25:40 reminds me that how I handle data belonging to the people I serve is not trivial — it is a matter of how I treat those Christ cares for.

Week 4 Addition

Essential Ethical Principles & Alternative Approach

Principle 1
Integrity
Acting in accordance with one's stated values and professional obligations even when no one is watching. The sysadmin violated this by choosing to misuse access they knew was not authorized for that purpose.
Principle 2
Responsibility
IT professionals are stewards of data and systems belonging to others. Responsibility means acknowledging that elevated access is a privilege granted to serve others — not a personal resource to be used at will.
Principle 3
Fairness
Students whose records were accessed did not have the opportunity to consent or object. Fairness demands that individuals are treated equitably and not subjected to surveillance or data access they have not agreed to.
Principle 4
Honesty
If the sysadmin had a legitimate reason to access those records, the ethical path was to request authorization through proper channels — not to act covertly. Honesty requires transparency about what you are doing and why.

Alternative Approach

A Better Way to Handle This

🔄 The Alternative: Proactive Governance Before the Violation Occurs

The most ethically sound alternative to this situation is not a better response after the fact — it is a governance framework that makes unauthorized access structurally difficult before anyone is tempted to attempt it. The original response (revoking access, launching an investigation, implementing stricter IAM policies) addressed the damage after it occurred. A principled alternative would have prevented the damage entirely through three proactive measures:

1. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) with Granular Scoping: Rather than granting the sysadmin broad administrative access to all institutional systems, the institution should have implemented RBAC policies that limited each administrator's access strictly to the systems their job function required. A sysadmin responsible for maintaining servers should have no access to student record databases — technically and architecturally. This is not just a policy change; it is an infrastructure design principle rooted in the ethical obligation of least privilege.

2. Continuous Access Audit Logging with Automated Anomaly Alerts: All access events — especially those by privileged users — should be logged to an immutable audit trail monitored by a SIEM system. Automated alerts should fire when an administrator accesses systems outside their normal operational scope. This creates both a deterrent (users know their actions are monitored) and a detection mechanism (unusual access is flagged immediately, not discovered weeks later). The ethical principle at work here is accountability — no professional should be able to act with impunity simply because they have technical access.

3. Ethics Training Integrated with Access Provisioning: Before any administrator is granted elevated access, they should be required to complete ethics training that explicitly covers data privacy obligations, FERPA compliance, and the consequences of unauthorized access — professional, legal, and personal. Access should be granted only upon signed acknowledgment of these obligations. This is the principle of informed consent applied to the professional context: the sysadmin should never be in a position where they can claim they did not understand the boundaries of their authorization.

✅ Why This Alternative Is Better — Outcomes for All Involved

The proactive governance alternative produces better outcomes for every stakeholder involved:

  • For students: Their private records are structurally protected, not merely policy-protected. They can trust the institution with sensitive data knowing that access is controlled by architecture, not just goodwill. Their dignity and privacy are respected as a matter of system design, not accident.
  • For the sysadmin: Clear boundaries protect them as much as they protect students. When access is granularly scoped, the temptation to overstep is reduced because the opportunity is reduced. A governance framework that makes unauthorized access difficult also makes it less likely that a professional — even one with poor judgment in a given moment — will make a career-ending mistake.
  • For the institution: Proactive governance dramatically reduces legal and reputational risk. A FERPA violation discovered after the fact carries penalties, public disclosure requirements, and lasting damage to institutional trust. Preventing the violation entirely through governance costs far less — financially and reputationally — than responding to it after it occurs.
  • For the IT profession: Every case of admin privilege misuse erodes public trust in IT professionals broadly. A culture of governance — where security and ethics are designed into systems rather than added as afterthoughts — elevates the entire profession and demonstrates that technical power and ethical responsibility can and must coexist.
Personal Application: As Technology Coordinator managing 50+ devices across 10 church units, I apply these exact principles daily. My access is scoped to what my role requires. My actions are transparent to the leaders I serve. And I operate under a self-imposed standard: if I would not be comfortable having my access logs reviewed by the people I serve, I should not be taking that action. Integrity is not a policy — it is a practice. — Bestman Ugochukwu Afokwalam, reflecting on D&C 58:27 and professional ethics
