Submission Item 2

Reflective Essay — Portfolio Alignment & Evolution

Where I Started and Where I Ended

When I began IT 497, my initial goal was straightforward: build a professional portfolio that would support my job search after graduation. I envisioned a site listing my projects, my resume, and perhaps some personal statement. What I did not anticipate was that the course would fundamentally reframe what a portfolio is for. By Week 2, I understood that a portfolio is not a marketing document — it is a testimony. It testifies not just of what you can do, but of who you are and why you do it.

That reframing changed every decision I made from that point forward. Instead of listing projects, I documented them — architecture diagrams, exact terminal commands, problems I debugged, lessons I learned. Instead of separating spiritual content into a sidebar, I integrated it into every project description as a spiritual reflection. Instead of writing a generic personal statement, I articulated three guiding principles rooted in specific scriptures and connected to specific professional experiences. The portfolio became a coherent narrative rather than a collection of items.

How Goals Changed and Why

My initial goal was breadth — show as many projects as possible. That goal evolved into depth — show fewer things but show them completely. The shift happened after Week 3's feedback from my instructor and from my own usability testing, which revealed that surface-level project descriptions were less impressive than honest, detailed ones. Emiliano Avila Betancourt's peer review confirmed this: the depth of my technical project descriptions was specifically cited as the portfolio's greatest strength.

A second goal that evolved was my approach to the spiritual content. I initially thought the spiritual and professional sections would remain somewhat parallel — faith on one side, technology on the other. What I discovered through five weeks of reflection is that they cannot be meaningfully separated. The discipline that built my CI/CD pipelines is the same discipline that built my prayer life. The integrity that protects church member data is the same integrity that protects student records in the ethical dilemma I analyzed. Elder Bednar's analogy of intertwined strands was not a metaphor I adopted for academic purposes — it became an accurate description of how I actually live.

Skills and Ethical Values Demonstrated

Technically, this portfolio demonstrates hands-on expertise in AWS multi-tier architecture, Google Kubernetes Engine, Docker container orchestration, CI/CD pipeline design, Terraform Infrastructure as Code, Azure DevOps, Ansible automation, and cloud monitoring with Datadog and Prometheus/Grafana. These are not hypothetical skills — each project page documents the exact commands, the exact problems encountered, and the exact solutions applied.

Ethically, the portfolio demonstrates a consistent framework: the Principle of Least Privilege applied to data access, FERPA compliance awareness, the duty of care to people whose data I manage, and an understanding of how algorithmic bias in data analysis can harm real people. These ethical commitments are rooted in spiritual convictions — Mosiah 2:17 on service, D&C 124:15 on integrity of heart, and Matthew 25:40 on treating every person as if serving Christ.

Future Use of This Portfolio

This portfolio will serve three distinct purposes going forward. First, as a job application tool — every role I apply for will receive this URL. The 17 pages of documented work, 11 published Medium articles, and real project detail pages provide evidence that would take a resume many pages to merely claim. Second, as a teaching resource — the portfolio guide I published as a PDF and DOCX document is already being shared with peers who want to build their own portfolios. My experience building this site became a resource for others, which is precisely the kind of service Mosiah 2:17 calls for. Third, as a living document — I will continue updating this portfolio as my career progresses, adding new projects, new certifications, and new reflections. A portfolio that stops growing at graduation is not a professional tool. It is a museum exhibit.
