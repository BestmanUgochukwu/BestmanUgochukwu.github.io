Task 1 — Spiritual Portfolio Enhancement

Christlike Attributes & Personal Warning Signs

The Savior said, "In your patience possess ye your souls" (Luke 21:19). This week's reflection challenged me to examine not just what I am becoming professionally, but who I am becoming spiritually — and to be honest about the warning signs that arise when those two dimensions fall out of alignment.

Christlike Attributes Woven Into My Life and Portfolio

Patience

Luke 21:19 — "In your patience possess ye your souls." Patience has been the most tested attribute in my IT journey. Deployments fail. Certifications require months of preparation. Career progress is slow when you are building real skills rather than collecting credentials. I have learned that patience in cloud engineering means trusting the process of small, consistent commits — both in code and in character.

Professional expression: Debugging a production issue at 2 AM without panic. Reading error logs carefully. Trying one fix at a time. That is patience applied to technology.

Humility

Proverbs 11:2 — "With the lowly is wisdom." Humility in IT means acknowledging what I do not know. I began my DevOps journey not knowing what a container was. I exposed AWS credentials in a chat window during a deployment. I asked for help from mentors, from peers, and from God. Each moment of humility opened a door to deeper learning. My portfolio documents not just what worked — it documents what failed and why, because humility means being honest about the full journey.

Professional expression: Every project detail page on this portfolio includes a "Problems I Debugged" section. That transparency is humility in practice.

Charity

Moroni 7:47 — "Charity is the pure love of Christ." Charity in my professional life looks like building the First Aid Hub for BYU Pathway students who struggle with platform navigation. It looks like mentoring two peers weekly not because it benefits my resume but because their success matters. It looks like showing full patience to the most frustrated Springboard student on a customer call, treating that interaction as what Mosiah 2:17 says it is — service to God.

Professional expression: The First Aid Hub was not assigned. It was built out of charity — seeing a need in others and acting on it freely.

Integrity

Doctrine & Covenants 124:15 — "I, the Lord, love him because of the integrity of his heart." Integrity in IT means the same thing it means in every other domain: your private conduct must match your public profession. As Technology Coordinator managing 50+ devices, I have access to data that belongs to the members I serve. I have never acted on that access beyond its authorized purpose, not because I was being watched, but because integrity does not have an audience.

Professional expression: The ethical dilemma of admin privilege misuse is not abstract to me. Integrity is the daily practice of choosing not to misuse what I have been given.

Diligence

Doctrine & Covenants 58:27 — "Anxiously engaged in a good cause." Diligence has produced every tangible achievement in my career. Five CompTIA certifications earned one chapter at a time. Two Champion of the Week awards earned by showing up fully every day of the internship. Eleven Medium articles documenting every project from scratch. A 3.91 GPA maintained across two degrees while working multiple jobs. Diligence is not a burst — it is a daily decision.

Professional expression: This portfolio itself is an act of diligence — 17 pages of documented work built incrementally over weeks.

Faith

Hebrews 11:1 — "Faith is the substance of things hoped for." I came to Ensign College from Nigeria, navigating a new country, a new educational system, and a technology field that was largely unfamiliar to me. Faith was not the absence of fear — it was the decision to act despite it. Every application, every certification attempt, every internship interview was an act of faith that the preparation would be honored and the path would open.

Professional expression: James 1:5 — asking God for wisdom before every major technical decision. Faith is my first tool, not my last resort.

Personal Warning Signs and Realignment Strategies

Recognizing warning signs — moments when my actions drift from my stated values — is itself a form of spiritual maturity. The following are honest observations about my own patterns and the strategies I use to realign:

⚠️ Warning Sign

Impatience During Slow Progress

When technical progress feels slow — a certification taking longer than expected, a deployment that will not cooperate — I notice impatience creeping in as frustration, shortcuts, and a willingness to skip documentation or testing. This is when my work quality drops and my spiritual temperature drops with it.

✅ Realignment Strategy

Pause, Pray, and Recommit

I have learned to recognize impatience as a signal to stop, pray, and recommit to the process rather than the timeline. Luke 21:19 is my anchor: "In your patience possess ye your souls." The soul is not possessed in moments of rapid success — it is possessed in the slow, faithful moments of continued effort.

⚠️ Warning Sign

Pride in Achievement

After earning the Champion of the Week award twice, I noticed a subtle shift — a temptation to lead with accomplishments in conversations rather than with genuine interest in others. Recognition, if not carefully anchored to humility and gratitude, can quietly convert service into self-promotion.

✅ Realignment Strategy

Return to Mosiah 2:17

Returning to Mosiah 2:17 and asking: "Am I in the service of others or in the service of my own reputation?" reorients me quickly. The award is a milestone, not an identity. My identity is a servant. I also practice sharing credit explicitly — crediting mentors, peers, and God whenever I discuss achievements publicly.

⚠️ Warning Sign

Neglecting Spiritual Habits When Busy

During intensive project sprints — particularly the CloudAdvisory internship and certification preparations — I noticed that daily prayer and scripture study became the first casualties of a packed schedule. When spiritual habits are skipped, technical decisions feel more anxious, leadership feels more transactional, and work loses its sense of meaning.

✅ Realignment Strategy

Treat Spiritual Habits as Non-Negotiables

I now treat prayer and scripture study the same way I treat production system uptime requirements — they are not optional features, they are core infrastructure. If I would not skip a scheduled deployment because I was busy, I should not skip my spiritual anchors for the same reason. The CI/CD pipeline runs daily. So does my spiritual maintenance.

Reviewing this portfolio as a cohesive whole, I can see that the integration of spiritual and professional content has deepened significantly across five weeks. Week 1 established the foundation — three guiding principles and initial spiritual materials. Week 2 added ethical analysis. Week 3 deepened the reflective activity and connected Bednar's intertwined strands directly to my professional competencies. Week 4 introduced agency (D&C 58:26–28) as the theological engine behind every career achievement. Week 5 — this week — adds Christlike attributes as the character framework underlying all of it.

The portfolio now reflects not just what I have built technically but who I am spiritually. Each project page includes a spiritual reflection. The cohesion I was seeking at the beginning of this course — a portfolio that does not separate the professional from the spiritual — is now present throughout.

The area requiring continued growth is deeper integration of Christlike attributes in daily work decisions, not just in reflective writing. The warning signs I documented above are real patterns I encounter regularly. The strategies are commitments, not completed achievements. That honesty is itself a Christlike attribute — standing in truth, as Sister Marriott counsels, even when the truth is that growth is still in progress.

Task 2 — Usability Testing & Design Enhancements

Portfolio Usability Testing Report

Usability testing was conducted across three devices (desktop browser, Android mobile phone, and tablet) and with two peer reviewers who navigated the site without guidance and reported friction points. The following documents the issues identified and the specific design improvements implemented.

Testing Methodology

Device 1
Desktop Chrome
Full navigation review, all project links tested, Medium article links verified
Device 2
Android Mobile Phone
Touch targets, nav visibility, button responsiveness, text readability
Peer Reviewer 1
Tech-Savvy Reviewer
Assessed project depth, technical accuracy, navigation flow
Peer Reviewer 2
Non-Tech Reviewer
Assessed clarity, readability, spiritual content visibility, overall impression

Issues Identified and Improvements Made

Issue Found

Navigation hidden on mobile

All navigation links were hidden on screens under 640px. Mobile users could not navigate between pages at all — a critical accessibility failure discovered during mobile testing.

Fix Implemented

Responsive nav with flex-wrap

Updated CSS to use flex-wrap on mobile so navigation links wrap into two rows rather than disappearing. All pages now navigable on any screen size.

Issue Found

Buttons unresponsive on mobile tap

Mobile browsers add a 300ms delay before registering a tap on links. The "View Résumé" and "View My Projects" buttons on the home page did not respond when tapped on mobile.

Fix Implemented

touch-action: manipulation added

Added touch-action: manipulation and -webkit-tap-highlight-color: transparent to all buttons, eliminating the delay and making every button respond instantly on tap.

Issue Found

Project cards not visually clickable

Both peer reviewers did not realise project cards were clickable. There was no visual indicator — no hover effect, no cursor change, no "View Project" call to action on the card itself.

Fix Implemented

Gold "View Full Project →" added to every card

Added a gold "View Full Project →" or "View Build Process →" text link at the bottom of every clickable project card. Added hover lift effect (translateY -3px). Users now understand immediately that cards are interactive.

Issue Found

Medium blog links all pointed to profile page

All 11 Medium article links on the Projects page and inside project detail pages pointed to the generic Medium profile URL rather than directly to each specific article. Reviewers had to search the profile to find the right article.

Fix Implemented

Each article links directly to its exact URL

Updated all 11 article links across the projects page and all project detail pages to point directly to the specific article URL. Every "Read Full Article on Medium →" button now opens the exact article instantly.

Issue Found

Missing project detail pages caused redirects to home

Several project cards linked to pages that did not exist yet. Clicking them redirected users to the home page instead of showing a 404, which was confusing — users thought they were on the wrong site.

Fix Implemented

All 10 project detail pages built and deployed

Built dedicated detail pages for all projects: Two-Tier AWS, MoodTunes GKE, Agentic DevOps, Azure Full Stack, Kubernetes Journey, Docker Bookstore, Terraform 3-Tier, Azure CI/CD, Ansible Automation, and the Portfolio Site itself. Each page includes architecture diagrams, step-by-step commands, problems debugged, and lessons learned.

Issue Found

Loom video demo not embedded — First Aid Hub

The First Aid Hub project had no visual demonstration. Reviewers could read about the app but could not see it in action, significantly reducing the project's impact and credibility.

Fix Implemented

Two Loom demo videos embedded directly

Embedded two Loom demonstration videos directly on the First Aid Hub project page using iframe embeds. Visitors can watch the full app demonstration without leaving the portfolio. Both demo links also open directly on Loom for fullscreen viewing.

Task 2 — Ethical Dilemma Reflection

My Personal View on the Ethical Dilemma

The ethical dilemma I have examined throughout this course — a system administrator accessing confidential student records without authorization — is not abstract to me. I serve as Technology Coordinator for The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, managing 50+ devices across 10 units. I have elevated access. I face the same daily choice the sysadmin in this case faced: to use access responsibly or to misuse it.

My Personal View

My view on this dilemma is unambiguous: the sysadmin's actions were wrong, and they were wrong at multiple levels simultaneously — ethically, professionally, legally, and spiritually. What makes this case particularly instructive is that the wrongness did not depend on anyone finding out. The action was wrong the moment it was taken, regardless of consequence. This is the most important ethical principle I take from this exercise: integrity is not defined by outcomes. It is defined by choices made when no one is watching and when no immediate consequence is visible.

What I find most sobering is not the violation itself — it is how easy it was. The sysadmin did not break any technical barrier. They used access they legitimately possessed, for a purpose it was not intended to serve. This is the nature of insider threat: it does not look like an attack. It looks like normal work. That is what makes governance frameworks, audit logging, and role-based access control not just technical best practices, but ethical necessities.

The FERPA framework exists precisely because students cannot protect their own records from the administrators who manage them. They must trust the institution. When that trust is violated, it is not just a policy failure — it is a betrayal of people who had no choice but to be vulnerable. As Christlike professionals, we are called to honor that vulnerability, not exploit it.

How I Would Handle Similar Situations in My Future Work

In my future IT roles — whether as a cloud security engineer, a systems administrator, or a DevOps lead — I will apply three concrete practices shaped by this ethical analysis. First, I will advocate for and implement role-based access control (RBAC) with the narrowest possible privilege scope in every environment I manage. The goal is to make unauthorized access architecturally difficult, not just policy-prohibited. Second, I will champion continuous access audit logging — not as surveillance, but as accountability infrastructure. Professionals who know their actions are logged behave differently than those who believe they are unwatched. Third, I will treat ethics training as a prerequisite for elevated access, not a formality completed after it is granted.

This exercise has deepened my understanding of ethical challenges in IT by showing me that the most serious ethical failures are often not dramatic — they are quiet. They happen in ordinary moments when a professional chooses convenience over constraint, or curiosity over covenant. My spiritual commitments do not allow me to separate who I am in the data center from who I am at church or in prayer. The same standard applies in both spaces. Doctrine and Covenants 124:15 says the Lord loves integrity of heart. That integrity must operate in server rooms as much as in temples.

A Life Consecrated — where every technical decision is a spiritual act, and every spiritual commitment produces professional excellence

In a quiet reflective space this week — in the spirit of attending the temple and pondering my covenants — I sat with a single question: What is the thread that runs through every meaningful achievement of the past year? The answer I kept returning to was not a technical skill, a certification, or an award. It was consecration — the decision to bring my whole self to every assignment, every role, every interaction, and offer it as something larger than career advancement.

President and Sister Kusch's counsel — "I tell you these things that ye may understand" — resonated deeply in this reflection. Understanding, not just performance, is the goal of an education. I have not just learned cloud technologies — I have come to understand why they matter, how they serve people, and what it means to build them with integrity. That understanding is the product of faith informing intellect, and intellect honoring faith.

Sister Marriott's call to "Stand in Truth" speaks to the professional moment I am entering. As I move from student to practitioner — from internship to career — I will face moments when the easiest path is not the most ethical one. Data will tempt curiosity. Access will tempt misuse. Competitive pressure will tempt corner-cutting. Standing in truth in those moments will require exactly the Christlike attributes I have documented above: patience with process, humility before uncertainty, charity toward the people my systems serve, integrity when no one is watching, and faith that righteous professional practice is itself a form of discipleship.

The mental map above is not a diagram of two separate systems that occasionally intersect. It is a map of one life — one integrated person who happens to work in technology and also happens to be a disciple of Jesus Christ. Bednar's strands are not metaphor. They are the actual architecture of how I live. My CI/CD pipeline runs on the same daily discipline as my prayer life. My commitment to data privacy runs on the same integrity as my temple covenants. My peer mentoring runs on the same love as my service to church members. They are the same rope. They always were.
